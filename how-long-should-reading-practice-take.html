How Long Should Reading Practice Take?

Reading practice does not need to be long to matter. For many beginning readers, a short, focused routine works better than a long session that ends in tears, bargaining, or exhaustion.

Ten calm minutes can be enough

A good reading session is not measured by how long your child sits at the table. It is measured by whether your child gets a small amount of useful practice and still feels willing to come back tomorrow.

For many families, ten focused minutes is a healthy target. That is enough time to warm up with one sound, read a few words, read one tiny story, and stop before the practice turns into a battle.

Simple version: Short and consistent beats long and miserable.

Why shorter practice helps

Beginning readers are doing a lot at once. They are looking at letters, remembering sounds, blending words, tracking a sentence, and trying to understand what they read. That is real work.

When practice goes too long, children may start guessing, rushing, refusing, or saying they hate reading. Often, the problem is not the child. The session has simply gone past the point where the child can learn calmly.

Use a predictable rhythm

The Beach Cat routine is designed to keep the target small and clear.

  • One sound: Say the sound together.
  • A few words: Read a small set of words with that sound.
  • One tiny story: Meet those words in a short story.
  • Done: Stop while reading still feels possible.

This routine gives your child enough repetition to learn without making the evening feel like homework.

What if your child wants to keep going?

If your child is happy and asking for more, that is wonderful. You can read another story, reread the same story, or let your child choose a favorite page.

Still, it is okay to stop early even on a good night. Ending with success teaches your child that reading time is safe, predictable, and not endless.

“You did it. Let’s stop while it still feels good. We can read another one tomorrow.”

What if ten minutes is too much?

Some children are tired after school. Some children are younger. Some are working hard to build attention, confidence, or speech and language skills. If ten minutes is too much, start with five.

A five-minute routine can still count:

  • Say one sound.
  • Read two or three words.
  • Read one sentence or one tiny story.
  • Celebrate and stop.

You can build from there as your child’s stamina grows.

Watch the child, not the clock

The clock is only a guide. The real signal is your child’s energy. If your child is still trying, listening, and recovering from small mistakes, keep going a little longer. If frustration is rising, make the next success the stopping point.

Signs it is time to stop

It may be time to end the session when your child starts to lose the ability to use the sounds they know.

  • Your child begins guessing every word.
  • Your child rereads the same word again and again without progress.
  • Your child becomes silly, angry, or tearful.
  • You feel yourself getting tense.
  • The story no longer feels like practice; it feels like a fight.

When this happens, help your child read one last word or sentence successfully, then stop.

Make the ending warm

The last minute matters. Try not to end with a lecture about trying harder. End by naming something your child did well.

“You found the sound.”
“You read that word from left to right.”
“You kept going even when it was tricky.”
“That is enough for tonight.”

What parents can do tonight

Choose one lesson from the Reading Path. Set a small goal before you start: one sound, three words, one tiny story. When your child finishes, stop and celebrate.
