what are high frequency words

High frequency words are words children see again and again in books, sentences, and everyday print. Learning to read them accurately and quickly helps early readers become more fluent.

High frequency words are the words that appear most often

Words such as the, and, is, to, you, and said show up frequently in children's books and everyday writing. Because children meet them so often, teachers usually give these words extra attention.

Simple definition: High frequency words are common words that appear very often in print.

Are high frequency words the same as sight words?

Not exactly. The two ideas overlap, which is why parents often hear them used as if they mean the same thing.

High frequency words

Words that appear often in written language. The label describes how common the word is.

Sight words

Words a reader recognizes instantly and accurately. The label describes what the reader can do.

A high frequency word can become a sight word after enough accurate practice. A word does not have to be irregular to become a sight word. Even fully decodable words such as in, it, at, and can may eventually be read automatically.

Many high frequency words can be sounded out

Children should not be taught to guess every common word from its shape. Many high frequency words follow phonics patterns that beginners can learn.

  • can, in, it, and up can be decoded with early letter-sound knowledge.
  • this, that, and with become easier after children learn the digraph th.
  • like, make, and came become easier after children learn silent e.

The best approach is to use phonics whenever possible, then give extra attention to the part of a word that is not yet predictable.

Some common words have a tricky part

Words such as said, was, one, and the contain spellings that may not match the patterns a beginning reader knows yet. These words still have useful sound information.

“This word is said. The s says /s/ and the d says /d/. The middle is the part we need to remember.”

This is more helpful than asking a child to memorize the whole word as a picture. It connects the word to letters and sounds while acknowledging the unusual part.

What are the first 100 high frequency words?

You may see lists called the first 100 high frequency words or the 100 high frequency words. These lists usually collect words that occur very often in early reading materials. Different programs may use different orders and slightly different words.

A list can be useful for planning, but it should not become a race. Children do not need to memorize all 100 words at once. They need a small, well-chosen group that matches the phonics patterns and stories they are currently reading.

For kindergarten and early readers: A few high frequency words at a time is enough. Quality of practice matters more than the size of the list.

How to teach high frequency words at home

Keep practice short, connected to sounds, and tied to real reading.

  • Choose two or three words from a story your child is about to read.
  • Ask your child to say the sounds they already know.
  • Point out the tricky part, if there is one.
  • Read the word in a short sentence.
  • Meet the word again in a tiny story.
  • Stop after a few successful minutes.

A simple five-minute routine

1. Read: Show the word and say it together.

2. Notice: Ask, “Which sounds do you know?”

3. Mark: Point out any tricky spelling.

4. Use: Read the word in a sentence.

5. Repeat later: Revisit it in tomorrow's story.

Repeated reading in meaningful sentences helps words become automatic. Flashcards can support practice, but they should not replace phonics, blending, and stories.

High frequency words in decodable stories

Decodable stories are built mostly from sound-spelling patterns a child has already learned. They also usually include a small number of high frequency words so the sentences sound natural.

For example, a beginning story might say:

Cat sat on the mat.
The sun is hot.

The words cat, sat, and mat may match the phonics lesson. Words such as the and is may be the high frequency words practiced alongside it.

How many should a child learn at once?

For most young readers, two to five words at a time is plenty. Add a new word only when the current set feels comfortable.

  • If your child is guessing, slow down.
  • If the words are becoming mixed up, reduce the set.
  • If practice feels tense, return to an easier story.
  • If your child reads the words smoothly in sentences, introduce one or two more.

What parents can do tonight

  • Choose three common words from your child's book.
  • Sort them into “easy to sound out” and “has a tricky part.”
  • Practice each word in one short sentence.
  • Read the page again so the words appear in context.
  • End while your child still feels successful.
The big idea: High frequency words are common words, not guessing words. Teach the sounds that are regular, explain the part that is tricky, and let repeated reading turn the word into a sight word.
