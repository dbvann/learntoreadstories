Letter Sounds vs. Letter Names: What Should Children Learn First?

Letter names tell children what a symbol is called. Letter sounds help them use that symbol to read a word. Children need both, but they do not have to master the entire alphabet before beginning to read.

The simple difference

The name of the letter M is “em.” One sound that M represents is /m/, as in moon, map, and mat.

Letter name

What the symbol is called: A, B, C, M, S.

Letter sound

The sound the symbol can represent inside a word: /m/ in moon or /s/ in sun.

A child who can sing the alphabet song may know many letter names. That is useful, but it does not automatically mean the child can look at mat, connect M with /m/, and begin sounding out the word.

For a fuller introduction to how sounds work in words, read What Are Letter Sounds?

Simple version: Letter names help children talk about letters. Letter sounds help children use letters to read.

Which should children learn first?

You do not have to choose one and ignore the other. Children can learn a letter’s name and its most useful sound together:

“This is M.”

“M can say /m/.”

“Moon starts with /m/.”

The important part is making sure sound practice is not delayed until every letter name is memorized. Reading begins when children connect printed letters with spoken sounds and start blending those sounds into words.

For that reason, if your child already recognizes some letters, spend more practice time on the sounds those letters make and on using those sounds in simple words.

Why the alphabet song is not enough

The alphabet song teaches order and familiarity. It helps children remember that letters belong to a set. But the song does not teach how the letters behave inside words.

A child may confidently say “B” while looking at the symbol and still need help hearing /b/ at the beginning of bat. That is normal. Naming a symbol and using it for reading are related skills, but they are not the same skill.

Start with useful, easy-to-hear sounds

Some sounds are easier to stretch and hear than others. Sounds such as /m/, /s/, /f/, /n/, and /r/ can be held briefly, which often makes them easier for a beginning reader to notice.

You can also begin with letters that appear in simple words your child can picture. A small group such as A, S, T, M, C, P, B, and R gives children many opportunities to meet useful sounds without racing through the alphabet.

The Level 1: First Words lessons follow this gentle approach: one letter, one sound, a few words, and one tiny story.

Should children learn uppercase or lowercase letters first?

Children should eventually recognize both. Uppercase letters are often easier to distinguish and appear in names and at the beginnings of sentences. Lowercase letters appear much more often in ordinary reading.

A practical approach is to show both forms when possible while keeping the sound consistent:

“This is M, and this is m.”

“They are both the letter M.”

“M can say /m/, like moon.”

There is no need to turn this into a test. Repeated exposure across short lessons will do more than one long drilling session.

What if my child knows sounds but forgets the names?

That is not a reason to stop reading practice. If your child sees M, says /m/, and uses that sound to begin reading mat, the child is doing important reading work even if the name “M” takes longer to remember.

You can gently provide the name and continue:

“Yes, that sound is /m/. The letter is called M. Let’s use /m/ to read mat.”

What if my child knows the names but not the sounds?

Choose one familiar letter and connect it to a concrete word. Keep the routine short:

  • Show the letter.
  • Say its name once.
  • Say one common sound clearly.
  • Practice two or three words using that sound.
  • Read a tiny story that repeats those words.

Avoid reviewing all 26 letters in one sitting. One successful connection is better than a tired alphabet quiz.

What parents can do tonight

Pick one letter your child already recognizes. Say the name, practice its sound, and use it in three words.

“This is S.”

“S can say /s/.”

“Sun, sand, and sit begin with /s/.”

Then open one Level 1 letter-sound lesson and stop while the experience still feels successful.

Remember: Children do not need perfect alphabet knowledge before reading begins. Letter names and sounds can grow together, one useful connection at a time.
