Letter Sounds for Kids: A Simple Parent Guide

Letter sounds are the sounds written letters can represent in words. When children connect those sounds to print, they can begin blending letters into words and reading for themselves.

What are letter sounds?

A letter has a name and can represent one or more sounds. The letter B is called “bee,” but in the word bat it represents the beginning sound /b/. That sound connection is one of the foundations of phonics and early reading.

Children do not need to memorize every possible alphabet sound before they start reading. It is more useful to learn a small number of common sounds, practice them in simple words, and then meet those words in a story.

Letter names and letter sounds are different

Letter names help children identify the symbols of the alphabet. Letter sounds help children use those symbols to read words. A child may know the alphabet song and still need practice connecting print to speech.

For example, the letter M is called “em,” but the useful reading sound in map, mat, and mug is /m/. The letter name identifies the symbol. The sound helps the child decode.

If you want a closer comparison, see Letter Sounds vs. Letter Names.

Common letter sounds for beginning readers

For early practice, beginning consonant sounds are often easiest to hear. These are examples of useful first sounds to practice:

B · /b/

bat, big

D · /d/

dog, dig

F · /f/

fish, fun

H · /h/

hat, hop

M · /m/

map, mug

P · /p/

pig, pet

S · /s/

sun, sit

T · /t/

top, ten

English is not perfectly one-letter, one-sound. Some letters can represent more than one sound. C, for example, sounds different in cat and city. G sounds different in go and gem. You do not need to teach every possibility at once. Teach the sound needed for the words your child is reading now.

Parent comfort: You do not have to explain all of English in one sitting. One useful sound is enough for today.

Short vowel sounds matter early

Short vowels appear in many simple CVC words, so they are especially useful when children begin blending sounds into words.

Acat, map
Ebed, pet
Ipig, sit
Odog, hop
Usun, cup

Once a child can hear several consonant and short-vowel sounds, they can begin pushing them together: c-a-t → cat, s-u-n → sun, and p-i-g → pig.

Try to keep consonant sounds clean

Adults sometimes add an extra “uh” when teaching a consonant. We might say “buh” for /b/ or “tuh” for /t/. Try to keep the sound brief when you can. Cleaner sounds are easier to blend because the child has fewer extra syllables to remove.

With bat, the goal is three compact sounds that can slide together: /b/ + short a + /t/.

How to practice letter sounds at home

You do not need flash cards for the whole alphabet. A short routine is easier to repeat and gives children a chance to use the sound for real reading.

1. Say one sound. “B can say /b/.”

2. Read a few words. “Bat. Big. Bed.”

3. Read one tiny story. Use a story or lesson that contains the same sound.

4. Stop while it still feels good. Reread once if your child wants to, then celebrate and finish.

Ten minutes is enough. Repetition matters more than making one practice session long.

Where letter sounds fit in the Reading Path

Letter sounds are already part of Level 1: First Words. Your child does not need a separate curriculum before beginning. Level 1 uses letter sounds, blending, short vowels, and simple words together so the sounds immediately have a purpose.

Ready to practice? Choose one Level 1 lesson. One sound. A few words. One tiny story.

What parents can do tonight

  • Choose one letter sound.
  • Say it clearly and briefly.
  • Read three to six simple words that use the sound.
  • Point to the letter as you hear the sound.
  • Read one tiny story or Level 1 lesson.
  • Stop before practice feels like a battle.
The big idea: Letter sounds for kids do not need to become an alphabet test. Help your child connect one sound to print, use it in a few words, and then read something meaningful.
