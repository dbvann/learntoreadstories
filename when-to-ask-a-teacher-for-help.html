When to Ask a Teacher for Help

You do not have to wait until reading feels like a crisis. A teacher can help you understand what is happening, what your child needs next, and whether extra support or screening would be useful.

Ask sooner than you think

If you are worried about your child's reading, it is okay to ask the teacher now. You are not bothering anyone. You are sharing useful information from home and asking whether the same patterns are showing up at school.

Many reading struggles are easier to support when adults notice them early. Reading Rockets notes that most children can become good readers when they get the right help, and the National Center on Improving Literacy explains that schools often use reading screening and tiered supports to identify children who may need extra help.

Simple version: If reading practice regularly ends in tears, guessing, avoidance, or confusion, send a short note to the teacher.

Signs it may be time to reach out

One rough reading night does not mean something is wrong. Look for patterns that repeat over time, especially after your child has had practice with the same skill.

Sounding out is very hard

Your child cannot remember common letter sounds, struggles to blend simple words, or guesses from the picture instead of looking through the word.

Reading feels unusually painful

Your child cries, shuts down, gets angry, complains of stomach aches, or avoids schoolwork when reading is involved.

Progress is not sticking

A word or sound seems learned one day, then disappears the next, even with short and patient practice.

School feedback worries you

Grades, test scores, reading group placement, or teacher comments suggest your child is falling behind or not catching up.

Specific reading signs to mention

When you contact the teacher, describe what you actually see. Specific examples are more helpful than saying, “My child is bad at reading.”

  • Mixing up similar letters or sounds again and again.
  • Guessing words from the first letter or the picture.
  • Reading very slowly, word by word, with little improvement.
  • Having trouble retelling what was just read.
  • Forgetting sight words that have been practiced often.
  • Strong spelling difficulty along with reading difficulty.
  • A family history of dyslexia or reading struggles.

The National Center on Improving Literacy lists warning signs such as trouble with speech sounds, word recognition, and understanding what is read. The International Dyslexia Association also points to difficulty with accurate or fluent word reading, spelling, and phonological processing as common signs connected with dyslexia.

What to say to the teacher

You do not need perfect educational language. A short, respectful message is enough.

“Hi, I wanted to ask about reading. At home, I’m noticing that reading practice is very hard for Sam. He often guesses words from the picture and has trouble blending sounds, even in short words. Are you seeing anything similar in class? What should we try next?”

If the teacher uses a term you do not know, ask for an example. Understood recommends being clear, specific, and asking follow-up questions so you understand what is happening in class and what can help at home.

Questions worth asking

  • “What reading skills are going well?”
  • “Which skills seem hardest right now?”
  • “Is my child reading at the expected level for this point in the year?”
  • “Has my child had a recent reading screening?”
  • “What extra support is available at school?”
  • “What should we practice at home, and what should we avoid?”
  • “When should we check back in?”

These questions keep the conversation practical. The goal is not to label your child. The goal is to understand the next step.

When to ask about screening or evaluation

Ask about screening or a fuller evaluation if your child is not making progress after targeted help, if the teacher is also concerned, or if the reading struggle is affecting confidence, school participation, or daily life.

A screening does not diagnose a child by itself. It helps identify risk and decide whether extra support or more assessment is needed. NCIL explains that reading screening helps schools identify students at risk for reading difficulties and can guide the level of support a child receives.

Helpful phrase: “Would a reading screening or evaluation help us understand what support my child needs?”

Bring notes from home

Before the meeting, write down three or four examples. Keep them plain and factual.

  • “She read cat yesterday but could not read it today.”
  • “He says the first sound, then guesses the rest of the word.”
  • “She can answer questions when I read aloud, but not when she reads the page herself.”
  • “Reading for ten minutes often turns into tears.”

These details help the teacher compare what happens at home with what happens in class.

Keep home practice gentle

While you wait for guidance, keep reading practice short and calm. Use one sound, a few words, and one tiny story. Stop before your child feels defeated.

If the teacher gives you a specific skill to practice, choose lessons from the Reading Path that match that skill.

What not to do

  • Do not wait months because you are afraid of overreacting.
  • Do not turn every night into a test.
  • Do not compare your child to siblings or classmates.
  • Do not assume a child is lazy because reading is hard.
  • Do not use harder books as the main solution.

Reading difficulty is not a character problem. It is information. Once adults understand the pattern, they can choose better support.

What parents can do tonight

Choose one short lesson your child can mostly read. Notice where the struggle happens: sounds, blending, sight words, fluency, or understanding. Then send one clear note to the teacher with your concern and one example.

The big idea: You do not need to diagnose the problem. You only need to notice the pattern, ask the teacher, and work together on the next step.
